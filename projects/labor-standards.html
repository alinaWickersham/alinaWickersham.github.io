Labor standards from decades of work orders

Developer · heavy equipment industry · 2026 – present

Problem

A heavy equipment dealer's technicians perform thousands of repairs a year. To quote a job, schedule a bay, or pay a technician fairly, you need a labor standard: how long this repair should take. The dealer had decades of completed work orders that contained the answer, but as unstructured text rather than numbers. In practice, standards were set by whoever had been there longest.

The requirement was not just a number. It had to be defensible — something a service manager could question and someone could answer.

Solution

A generation pipeline over the historical archive in Snowflake that produces a labor standard for each job, with citations pointing back to the specific records it drew from. Deterministic validation rules run after generation to catch outputs that are internally inconsistent or outside plausible bounds, so the model is never the last word.

Subject matter experts review the results in a purpose-built app before anything is accepted, and approved standards export as XML directly into the systems the dealer already uses. An evaluation framework measures accuracy as the pipeline changes.

What I built

  • Work on the planning side of the system, alongside the standards generation itself.
  • Direct contact with the client's stakeholders on requirements and open questions.

Stack

Snowflake, Go, a citation-backed generation pipeline, deterministic validation rules, XML export into the client's existing workflow, a hosted review application for subject matter experts, and an accuracy evaluation framework.

What was interesting

This is a problem where a plausible answer is worse than no answer. A labor standard that looks reasonable but is wrong propagates into quotes, schedules, and pay. So the design question was never "can a model produce a number" — it was "how does a person check the number without redoing the work."

Citations and provenance are the answer to that, and the review app is where it becomes real: the expert sees the standard next to the records behind it, and approves or rejects with the evidence in front of them. The deterministic validation catching what the model gets wrong is the other half. Together they are the reason this can be trusted in a workflow where money depends on it.
