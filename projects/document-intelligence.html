Document intelligence platform for family offices

Architect and lead · concept to beta · 2026 – present

Problem

Family offices manage money for a small number of families, and almost every document that crosses their desk is dense with personal detail: names, account numbers, addresses, holdings. Two things follow from that. Staff extract the figures they need by hand, because the documents arrive in dozens of formats and no two look alike. And none of those documents can be handed to a language model as they are, because the personal information would leave the building.

So the work stays manual, and the obvious automation is closed off by the privacy problem rather than the technical one.

Solution

A platform that solves the privacy problem first and the extraction problem second. Documents pass through a redaction service that identifies personal information locally, before anything reaches a model, and replaces it with consistent pseudonyms. Because the substitution is offset-based, the original values can be restored precisely afterward, so the output is useful rather than anonymized into uselessness. Every step is written to an audit log.

With the document safe to process, extraction pulls the financial figures into structured records that downstream systems can use.

What I owned

  • Architecture and technical direction from the first conversation.
  • A large share of the implementation, and review of the rest.
  • Delivery planning and coordination across a core team of two to three, scaling to nine engineers to hit the beta deadline.
  • The client relationship — requirements, tradeoffs, and what shipped in which order.
Idea → beta Taken from concept to a working product now in beta testing

Stack

React and Vite on the front end. A Python redaction service using a local entity recognition model, with offset-based pseudonymization and audit logging. Azure OpenAI on a private endpoint. PostgreSQL, Azure Blob Storage, Alembic for migrations.

What was interesting

The design constraint that shaped everything was that identification had to happen locally, before any external call. That rules out the easy approach of asking a large model to find the personal information for you, and it makes the local model's recall the ceiling on the whole system's safety. Most of the hard thinking went into that boundary: what happens when the redactor misses something, how to make its failures visible, and how to prove after the fact what was sent where.

Scaling the team from three to nine for a deadline was its own problem. Work that had lived in a few heads had to become legible quickly, which meant writing down decisions that had been implicit and deciding what was safe to parallelize.
